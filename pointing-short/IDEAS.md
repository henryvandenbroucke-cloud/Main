## 1. Who does this style, and what the numbers say
- **Only one channel really owns this look: Rick's Clips (@RicksClips3000).** It is the channel the skill measured. Its 29 story shorts since 7 Oct 2026 (yt-dlp, 14 Nov 2026):
  - Top: The Ancient Jar That Warned an Empire 34M, Why Perfect Railroads Don't Work 28M, The Job Nobody Wants in Japan 18M, Why Sailors Carried Storm Oil 16M, Your Brain's Secret Idea Zone 16M, The Window That Breaks Japan's Gambling Law 15M.
  - Newest three: How Mine Salting Worked 2.7M (1 day old), How One Cart Stopped an Entire Army 5.5M (3 days), Why Drivers Started Filming The Road 4.3M (6 days).
  - Flops: The Science Behind Slot Machines 136K, How Painkillers Actually Work 320K, Every Memory Has a Price 543K. All three are general "how X works" explainers with no person, place or paradox.
- **Copycats so far are tiny.** Remakes posted on X got 47-494 likes. YouTube search found stick-figure clones of the emu war with 5-130 views. Nobody else has a hit in this exact look yet.
- **Neighbouring formats:** Zack D. Films (3D shorts, about 28M subscribers) and its clones (NYKentertain, Zeck Felms) own "realistic 3D explainer". ConChaser owns the long "What If You Could See..." stories (the idea behind `future-job-short/`).
- **What wins on the reference channel:** one specific true story with a place, an object or a person, and a paradox. Japan (18M and 15M), lottery and gambling loopholes (four videos, 6.5M-15M), old objects with a hidden purpose, and counterintuitive engineering.
- **Length:** 58-70 s is the sweet spot (10 of the top 15).

## 2. What the tutorials say about the hook and the script
Sources: the skill's own style bible (20 shorts measured), the revid.ai breakdown of this channel, the transcript of "I Tried Making Viral Cartoon Shorts Using ONLY Claude" (ReachMora), and the summary of Mr omi's workflow video. They agree on almost everything:
1. **Hook = one plain sentence about something that makes no sense, with the reason hidden.** 9-16 words, inside the first 3 seconds, no date, no setup. "The first line is something that can't be true."
2. **The first frame is the thumbnail.** The strange act must be visible in frame 1, and the voice starts in frames 1-4.
3. **Setup** (3-12 s): who, where, one exact number.
4. **A mocker** laughs at the hero with one short line. The hero stays calm.
5. **Escalation**: it gets stranger three times.
6. **"You see,"** then the trick, explained once through something the viewer has done.
7. **Payoff**: the last line flips the first line, with no moral after it. "That's what makes people watch it twice."
- Voice: one fast, steady narrator, 3.5-4.1 words per second, about 240 words in 65 s. The camera never stops moving.
- General Shorts advice (vendor blogs, so treat the numbers as rough): aim to hold about 80% of viewers through second 3, and never pay off the hook in the first 5 seconds.

## 4. The pick: idea 1
**Working title:** Why Japanese Train Drivers Point at Nothing

**Hook options** (A is my choice):
- A. "Japan pays train drivers to point at nothing and shout at nobody." (12 words)
- B. "In Japan, train drivers point at empty air all day, and they're in trouble if they stop." (17 words)
- C. "This man points at nothing every few seconds, and it keeps millions of people alive." (15 words; the claim is harder to defend)

**Beat plan (about 62 s, 235 words):**
1. Hook A. Picture: the driver already pointing in frame 1 (this is the preview clip).
2. Setup: a Japanese platform, a train arriving to the second, white gloves.
3. Mocker: a kid behind the yellow line. "Who's he talking to?"
4. Escalation: he points at the signal, the clock, the speedometer, then the empty track.
5. "You see," your brain runs boring jobs on autopilot. Ever locked your door and could not remember doing it a minute later?
6. Pointing and saying it out loud makes eyes, arm, mouth and ears all check the same thing. In a 1994 test, mistakes fell by almost 85%.
7. Payoff: New York copied it in 1996. Its conductors must point at a striped board at every stop. Riders found out and started holding up signs under the board. Last line flips the hook: "So now New Yorkers hold up signs just to get pointed at."

**Facts and sources:**
- Pointing and calling (shisa kanko) began on Japanese railways in the early 1900s with steam crews calling signals to each other. Pointing was added a few decades later. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointing_and_calling)
- A 1994 Railway Technical Research Institute study found it "reduced mistakes by almost 85 percent when doing a simple task". [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointing_and_calling) Other sources give 0.38 mistakes per 100 actions with both steps, and "one-sixth". [IEEE Pulse](https://www.embs.org/pulse/?p=476), [Nippon.com](https://www.nippon.com/en/articles/-/16478)
- New York: conductors point at a black-and-white striped board before opening the doors. Wikipedia says the procedure started in 1996 after doors opened in tunnels. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointing_and_calling)
- 2013: Yosef Lerner and Rose Sacktor stood under the boards with signs such as "Point here if you are dead sexy", and conductors pointed. [ABC News](https://abcnews.go.com/blogs/lifestyle/2013/10/pranksters-give-nyc-subway-conductors-a-dead-sexy-surprise/)
- Not confirmed: the "2.38 mistakes per 100 without it" figure, and the story of one named Kobe driver starting it. Do not use either until a second source is found. The Atlas Obscura article that carries them could not be opened from this server.
- The script must say "mistakes in a test", not "accidents".
