- Frame 1 already shows the absurd action.

## Facts (sources)
- Wheeltappers checked train wheels by tapping them with a long-handled hammer and listening: a sound wheel rings,
  a cracked one sounds dull (like a cracked bell). They also checked that axle boxes were not overheating.
  Steam-era job at big stations and goods yards; made redundant by planned maintenance and defect detectors.
  (Wikipedia: Wheeltapper; Science Museum Group: wheeltapper's hammer)
- Automated trackside systems strike passing wheels and analyse the sound ("acoustic signature" cracked-wheel
  detection). (US patent 4,402,210; FRA automated cracked wheel detection)

## Voice script (ElevenLabs eleven_v4, voice "Aaron Gerard"; tags are directions, not spoken)
[excited] This man's whole job... was hitting trains with a hammer.
[mischievously] And if one wheel made the wrong sound, that train wasn't going anywhere.
[curious] It's the 1900s. Busy station, steam everywhere. And there he is. Ting. Ting. Ting.
[laughs] Passengers think he's lost his mind. Kids copy him.
[whispers] Then one wheel goes... [dramatically] THUNK.
[excited] And that carriage is pulled right off the train.
[surprised] Wait... how does a hammer find a broken wheel?
[whispers] You see, a solid steel wheel rings like a bell. [excited] But if it has a crack, even one you can't see, the ring dies instantly.
[curious] Ever flicked a wine glass? Ting. A cracked one just goes... thud.
[dramatically] And a cracked wheel at full speed could shatter, and derail the whole train.
So he listened to every single wheel, and touched every axle box, to feel if it was running hot.
[warmly] Today, the wheel tappers are gone.
[mischievously] But trains still get tapped. Machines beside the track hit every wheel as it rolls past... and listen.
[laughs] The hammer man just became a robot.
