# "The Man Who Made Bricks Too Big" — original script v1 (2026-11-06)
Format: ~60 s vertical animated story short, cartoon characters in a painted 3D toon town, fast emotional narration.

## Facts (sources)
- Britain taxed bricks 1784-1850 (Duties on Bricks and Tiles Act 1784, to help pay for the American war), charged per
  thousand bricks before firing (2s 6d per 1,000 at first); moulds carried the word "excise". (Wikipedia: Brick tax)
- Brickmakers answered by making bigger bricks. Joseph Wilkes of Measham, Leicestershire, began double-sized bricks in
  1785 to halve his tax; they are known as "Wilkes' gobs" / "Measham gobs". (Wikipedia: Joseph Wilkes; Historic England)
- 1801 (confirmed 1803): maximum brick size fixed at 10 x 5 x 3 inches; bigger bricks paid double duty. (Wikipedia; Scottish Brick History)
- Tax repealed 1850 (Brick Duties Repeal Act 1850). (Wikipedia)
- Buildings of Wilkes' gobs survive in Measham, e.g. Brickyard Cottages on Bosworth Road. (Historic England)

## Voice script (ElevenLabs eleven_v4, voice "Aaron Gerard"; tags are performance directions, not spoken)
[excited] In 1784, England put a tax on every single brick. [mischievously] So one guy... just made his bricks TWICE as big.
[dramatically] Britain had just lost America, and it was BROKE. So somebody in London had a genius idea: [sarcastic] count the bricks.
[excited] Every thousand bricks you bake? Pay up. They even stamp the word "excise" on them, so nobody cheats.
Meet Joseph Wilkes. He makes bricks in a little town called Measham.
He looks at the tax man. [short pause] He looks at his brick mould. [mischievously] And he doubles it.
[excited] His bricks come out FAT. Same wall, half the bricks, half the tax!
[laughs] People still call them Wilkes' gobs.
[curious] The tax man counts the wall. Counts it again. [surprised] Wait... why is this house so cheap?
[whispers] You see, the law said "per brick". [excited] It never said how BIG a brick had to be!
Soon other brickmakers start baking chunky bricks too, [angry] and London is FURIOUS.
[dramatically] So the government strikes back: ten inches long, five wide, three high. Anything bigger? [shouting] DOUBLE tax.
[sighs] Wilkes' trick is dead. The tax hangs around for decades, until 1850, when they finally give up and scrap it.
[warmly] But Wilkes' giant bricks? More than two hundred years later, they're still standing in Measham. [mischievously] Still the size of a tax dodge.
